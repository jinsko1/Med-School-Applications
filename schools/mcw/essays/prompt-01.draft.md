# Diversity / Community Contribution Shared Draft

He had sketched a rough design in his notebook for the tattoo he hoped to get once released. When I asked about its aspects—partly derived from those already on his arm—he confided that the text, written in a barbed font, was his daughter’s name.

He is seventeen; his daughter is three.

Among the youth I encountered in juvenile hall, I was most struck by the lived difference between myself and “A.” He had endured what no child deserves to endure, and had been convicted of attempted murder—the charge that brought him there. Yet in casual conversation, one would never discern such a grave history.

Though difficult to admit, perhaps somewhere within myself, I had presumptuously assumed him to be somehow inherently different; so different from me that I could hardly comprehend the distance between us. Yet, by degrees, I came to know him as a ferocious player of Egyptian War—as I learned through repeated losses—who spent his solitude writing lyrics, which he would show me when I volunteered. Gradually, through card games, conversations, and his art, I came to know him beyond his charge.

This challenged the way I understood circumstance and character. Had I been born into a childhood as disagreeable as “A’s,” even if I were fundamentally myself, my life could have gone astray. My fortunate and privileged circumstances were purely due to chance, not some inherent virtuous quality I possess.

Of course, this does not mean I am capable of fully understanding him. It would be presumptuous to think so. But it is important to remember that we are all fundamentally human, beings who hurt and rejoice in diverse, appreciable ways.

After turning these thoughts over in my mind, I began approaching him in a way that I hope came across as more keen and open. I began asking him about his past, not to unveil a mystery for my own sake, but to better understand the person behind my first impression. Slowly but surely, the distance between us began to close. Now, I consider him one of my closest friends.

Moving forward, I hope to carry this lesson into medicine: that no person should be reduced to the most visible or troubling part of their history. By asking questions and resisting the urge to form biases, I hope to help bridge social distances that may first seem unimaginable.
