# Wake Forest University School of Medicine - Commitment Draft

Many of the youth I encountered in juvenile hall had lived without a reliable sense of stability. Oftentimes, the closest thing to constancy came from friends and from the people who continued to show up for them. As such, I committed myself to volunteering consistently, knowing that trust could not be built through occasional presence alone. Had I appeared only sporadically, I would have remained another transient volunteer, someone they had little reason to trust or open themselves to.

Over time, I learned that commitment required more than simply arriving each week. It required active engagement: asking questions, holding conversations, and making the effort to know each youth as an individual. When they invited me to play basketball or showed me their artwork, I understood these moments as small signs of trust. They were not merely activities, but openings into friendship.

Commitment is both consistency and attention. To commit oneself to others is not only to be physically present, but to continue showing up with the willingness to listen and earn trust gradually.
