# AMCAS Work and Activities Draft

## 1. Microbiology Undergraduate Researcher

**Category:** Research/Lab

In Dr. Alejandra Yep's lab, I investigate iron acquisition and motility in uropathogenic *E. coli* as a potential therapeutic strategy for treating urinary tract infections. Since my first year, I have performed bacterial culture work, growth curves, motility assays, literature review, and data analysis. As my responsibilities increased, I trained new undergraduate researchers in laboratory protocols and troubleshooting. I later began pursuing more independent projects, including investigating surfactant effects on motility and developing software to improve experimental workflows for other research laboratories at Cal Poly.

**Most Meaningful Reflection**

I remember vividly the many times I had to re-perform growth curves over an entire summer. Wells dried up; controls became contaminated; strains that were supposed to grow simply refused to. Additions to the protocol throughout the summer often meant repeating experiments I thought were finished. I was, generously put, somewhat irritated, to the point that I occasionally blamed my own failures on the innocent bacteria, and yes, I am ashamed of this. Research already seemed elusive, with no clear path between the start and the goal, and its repetition only made the uncertainty more frustrating.

And yet Dr. Yep's patience, and even more so the tenacity I observed in my peers, changed how I understood research. Often, it is tedious, sometimes even menial, but the persistent attempt despite one's own frustration is what propels science forward. This realization brought my corroding relationship with bacteria, we're still working things out, into the research facility's reading room, where I explained my problems to peers from other labs. Their questions interrupted my usual ways of thinking and gave me new approaches to old problems. In turn, I found myself doing the same for them. Research became less solitary, less procedural, and more a shared effort to think carefully through uncertainty.

## 2. Chemistry Undergraduate Researcher

**Category:** Research/Lab

In collaboration with Dr. Corrine Lehr's lab, I worked to quantify intracellular iron concentrations in isogenic *E. coli* strains using atomic absorption spectroscopy. Since our project relied on assumptions about intracellular iron levels, we sought empirical evidence under the conditions we were interested in. I studied the mechanisms and limitations of AAS while helping develop protocols for sample preparation and analysis. This experience strengthened my interest in analytical chemistry and taught me how science involves constant questioning of one's own assumptions.

## 4. Clinical Assistant

**Category:** Paid Clinical Experience

As a Clinical Assistant at the Cal Poly Health Center, I supported student patient care by taking vitals, rooming patients, cleaning exam rooms, collecting urine samples, and transporting injured students back to their dorms or apartments. This role taught me how much of good care depends on small, meticulous, but meaningful actions. I remember assisting a student who was crying in the hallway while holding an emesis bag. Instead of rushing the rooming process, I told her to take her time and helped her slowly to the exam room. Through this experience, I learned that patient comfort often begins even before the physician enters.

## 5. Hospice Volunteer

**Category:** Community Service/Volunteer - Medical/Clinical

As a volunteer with Hospice SLO County, I have visited an elderly hospice patient biweekly since the summer after my first year to provide companionship and support during end-of-life care. Even after her transition into a senior home, I continue visiting her regularly. Working with a patient experiencing dementia taught me that communication often depends less on direct conversation and more on careful observation. Over time, I became more aware of how consistency and patience can impact a patient's quality of life.

**Most Meaningful Reflection**

Frankly, Beverley was not the client I expected to volunteer for. During training, I had requested to work with seniors capable of coherent conversation. So, as one might imagine, I was much surprised to meet Beverley for the first time. She could respond to simple questions, but anything more seemed beyond her cognitive capabilities. Her gaze was perpetually distant, as if searching for something absent. Hoping to build a strong relationship with hospice clientele, I was, though I now find this ridiculous, somewhat disappointed. I tried speaking with her as much as I could, to get to know her as much as I could. But this often tired her out, as did the physical therapy exercises we did together despite her reluctance. In retrospect, I realized I might have imposed myself upon her.

After many visits, I started noticing small habits in her language and movements: the way she limped, almost unnoticeably, on one leg; the way she asked, elusively, to go on walks; the way she stared out onto the street. Through these seemingly meaningless observations, I found I could get to know her beyond what could be said with words. Hospice taught me that care does not always begin with conversation or even understanding; sometimes it begins by learning how another person communicates before deciding what they need.

## 6. Restorative Partners Volunteer

**Category:** Community Service/Volunteer - Non-Medical

After completing restorative justice training with Restorative Partners, I began visiting juvenile hall weekly to support the incarcerated youth through playing sports, cards, and casual conversation. These visits became a consistent way to develop genuine camaraderie and learn about their experiences, many of which involved poverty and limited opportunity. Over time, I became more aware of how strongly social conditions shape behavior. This experience has made me more conscious of my own privilege and strengthened my interest in working with underserved populations in medicine.

**Most Meaningful Reflection**

There's often an unnamed tension as new volunteers stream in. It is as though the incarcerated youth tacitly signal: I did not ask to be helped. And this is natural; what could privileged individuals from outside understand about their experience? The first time I volunteered, these conflicts rattled my mind. If I was perpetuating some paternalistic agenda, there was no point in me being there.

Yes, they have committed crimes, some ghastly. Yet fundamentally, they are my peers in so many surprising ways. I learned that speaking casually with them, often while sharing food or playing cards, develops trust between people whose experiences are nothing alike. Expressing the willingness to learn about their lives, thoughts, and feelings led to friendships worth remembering. Many come from worlds I have never had to inhabit, cursed by poverty and failed institutions. Getting to know them led me to discover their love of art, especially lyrics and poetry, and their willingness to share it. After several discussions with staff, I convinced the facility to publish their art in weekly newsletters sent to donors, volunteers, and guardians.

I want to work with underserved populations in medicine because care begins not with the presumptuous assertion that I understand, but with remaining present long enough to try.

## 7. Learning Assistant

**Category:** Teaching/Tutoring/TA

As a Chemistry Learning Assistant, I worked with faculty to facilitate problem-solving in a general chemistry classroom. My role was not to simply give students answers, but to help them reason through where their understanding had failed, one-on-one. I supported students during class, graded assignments after class, and prepared weekly for prospective lab experiments with other learning assistants and instructors. Teaching made me receptive to how different students learn in different ways, and how patience, trial-and-error, and empathy are essential for building a welcoming learning environment.

## 8. Physician Mentorship / Shadowing

**Category:** Physician Shadowing/Clinical Observation

Through the Dignity Health Physician Mentorship Program, I rotated through nephrology, pediatrics, neonatology, orthopedic surgery, and anesthesiology over a summer. I observed clinic visits, patient histories, a delivery, knee and hip replacements, and perioperative care. I brought questions to clinic to discuss with physicians to better understand their reasoning. The experience overall has consolidated my interest in medicine.

## 9. ASI University Student Governor

**Category:** Leadership

During my term, I identified the lack of menstrual products in student-government-run facilities and worked with two peers to research, propose, budget, and implement sanitary pads in campus restrooms. This project, which at first seemed distant and unrealistic, made student advocacy feel much more attainable. A basic need still required planning, budgeting, and coordination before it could become an actual change in the spaces students use every day. Overall, it was quite a bit of fun working with my fellow ASI members to achieve a common goal.

## 13. Scientific Software Development

**Category:** Other / Research

After repeated conversations in lab about how manual swim-diameter measurements and colony counting slowed data analysis, I developed AgarLens, a desktop app for analyzing agar plate images. The program measures growth diameter and area from scanned plates, counts bacterial colonies using a YOLO model, and exports annotated images for analysis. Making the tool allowed me to treat a laboratory inconvenience as a fun computational problem. It also gave the project a use beyond my own data, since the code is public and can be used or modified by other labs. The project can be found here: <https://github.com/jinsko1/AgarLens>, and I am planning on officially publishing it through a software journal.

## 14. Academic Website / Educational Resource Development

**Category:** Other

I created and maintain a personal academic website, <https://jinsko.com>, as a way of genuinely understanding what I am doing in the lab rather than simply following procedures. Writing about the projects forces me to explain the background, mechanisms, and questions behind my work, which often exposed gaps in my understanding, leading to more literature review. If I could not explain an idea clearly, I did not understand it well enough.

## 15. Hobbies / Personal Interests

**Category:** Hobbies/Avocations

As a hobby, I read widely, from inexpensive mass-market paperbacks to textbooks, though I am most drawn to novels. Beyond the pleasure of reading itself, fiction offers me a practical benefit: it lets me inhabit minds unlike my own. In that exchange of consciousness, the boundaries of the self become less rigid, more porous. Reading has made me more comfortable with ambiguity, more attentive to perspective, and more receptive to different ways of thinking. Among some of my favorites are *Great Expectations*, *Frankenstein*, *1984*, and *Bel Canto*. For medicine specifically, I would recommend works by Atul Gawande. Any article or textbook by David Sackett is fantastic.
