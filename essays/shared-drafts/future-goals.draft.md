# Future Goals Shared Draft

Over the next decade, I hope to see the field evolve into a more inclusive form of evidence-based medicine, one that advocates for addressing the lack of research in certain populations, thus questioning whom interventions work for, addressing, in particular, the disconcerting lack of research in women’s health.

My research in UTIs revealed this disparity, as I found the literature concerning UTIs in women shamefully sparse. On top of the myriad of health problems that post-menopausal women face, recurrent urinary tract infections cause significant discomfort and economic burden yet remain understudied, revealing a desperate need for trials that evaluate the treatment and prophylaxis of UTIs in women.

The biased evidence base is especially true of clinical trials, and to imagine that many providers base their care on skewed populations appalls me. I find rectifying these gaps in medicine a priority in the coming years and therefore hope to see a more inclusive form of healthcare that serves patients equitably.

### Like Best

Surely, the best part of being a physician is the lifelong intellectual endeavor the profession demands. As the literature changes and standards of care shift, one must learn to be critical, keep up with one’s field, and recognize where knowledge is insufficient. I already find enjoyment in this process as I read clinical trials when I can, writing about topics that interest me, such as sham surgeries, drug classes, and surrogate endpoints. As a physician, it becomes imperative that I pursue questions not simply assigned to me. Medicine would allow this habit to become a responsibility rather than a mere interest.

Additionally, beyond purely furthering my own medical education, I look forward to the intersection of intellectual work and human experience. When reading fiction, one participates in an exchange of consciousnesses, temporarily occupying another mind. In medicine, this attempt to understand another person becomes ever more pertinent and consequential. Patients bring experiences, social circumstances, and perspectives hitherto unknown, and the physician must listen carefully while also thinking clinically.

I think I will find fulfillment in that medicine asks for both self-directed learning and receptiveness to unknown, sometimes even estranging perspectives. It is a never-ending intellectual endeavor, but one grounded in service.

### Like Least

Should I have the fortune to practice medicine in underserved communities, I acknowledge that many manifestations of illness are mere facades for more systemic, underlying causes that stretch their roots to the social determinants of health. Knowing this, as a future physician, I may not possess the power to immediately alleviate a patient’s pain, begotten from deeper, more sinister conditions; instead, I may only be able to treat the visible, superficial consequences.

All this logically follows, but facing it in person, with a real patient before me, is, I imagine, an incredibly disheartening feeling. Drawing from what little I know, that would most likely be my least favorite aspect of being a physician.

That being said, this only turns my attention to advocacy and strengthens my will to change the political infrastructure that allows such mortal inequities to persist.

### Goals

As I say, as a future physician, I’d like to practice medicine in underserved communities, where need is greatest but care is often sparse. Much of disease has been produced far before the encounter in the clinical setting. I hope to treat disease while remaining conscious of the underlying structures that exacerbate it: poverty, unstable housing, stigma, and limited access to care.

I may never fully understand a patient’s lived experience, and it would be presumptuous to claim otherwise. But I can ask questions carefully, resist the urge to form biases, and recognize that circumstance often masquerades as character. If medicine often takes place downstream, I hope to spend my career asking what can be done upstream.

### Why Eastern Virginia

A medical education at VHS-EVMS at ODU agrees with what I have learnt through service and research, allowing me to become the kind of physician I hope to be: one who is constantly aware of the community and attentive to the social determinants of health, while conducting clinically relevant research.

I much appreciate the curriculum, which places many pertinent experiences early in medical education. Training in a Level 1 trauma facility is an uncommon opportunity and would expose me to the breadth of medicine, and therefore to the different social realities real patients face.

Further, my years conducting UTI research have made me want to continue asking pertinent, puzzling questions. VHS-EVMS’s medical student research opportunities would allow me to preserve this habit of inquiry while staying grounded in service.

### Diversity

My service in juvenile hall taught me how easily a person can be reduced to the most visible or troubling part of their history—and therefore prepared me to work with diverse environments. Among the youth I encountered, I was most struck by the lived difference between “A” and me. He had endured what no child deserves to endure, and yet, in casual conversation, one would never discern such a grave history.

Though I am ashamed to admit, perhaps somewhere within myself, I had presumptuously assumed him to be somehow inherently different; so different from me that I could hardly comprehend the distance between us. Yet, by degrees, through card games, conversations, and his art, I came to know him beyond his charge. This challenged the way I understood circumstance and character. Had I been born into a childhood as adverse as “A’s,” even if I were fundamentally myself, my life could have taken a very different path. My fortunate circumstances were purely due to chance, not some inherent virtuous quality I possess.

Through medical school, I hope to continue learning how social variables shape health. I hope to carry this lesson into medicine: that no person should be reduced to the most visible part of their history. By asking questions and resisting the urge to form biases, I hope to provide care that is equitable, inclusive, and grounded in the dignity of the human before me, while sharing these virtues with my peers.

Further, my love for reading—fiction and nonfiction—serves as an intellectual praxis. When reading fiction, one participates in an exchange of consciousnesses, as it were, temporarily occupying another mind. In this state, the reader is introduced to new experiences, social circumstances, and perspectives hitherto unknown. I believe this makes me more receptive to new ideas, more understanding of others, and more culturally aware, therefore making me a better peer for my fellow medical students.
